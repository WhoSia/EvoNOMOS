## Restored publication thesis

**Working title:** *From Design Advice to Apparent Axioms: The Principle-to-Patch Transport Problem in AI-Assisted Software Review*.

Paper B's ambition is to **name a problem and propose a disciplined research program**:
- *Over-axiomatization*: the error of promoting conditional design advice or its automated label into an unconditional reason to approve/reject one patch without identified scope, requested future change, authorized compatibility contract and permitted repair route.
- *Principle-to-patch transport*: a conceptual name proposed here for a gap between evidence that a design principle classifies source structure and evidence that it justifies a specific engineering action. This label is **proposed language**, not a new theorem, nor proof that equivalent problems were unstudied.
- *Authority-bearing context*: a route from actual issue/request/API/source-owner statements to the requirements under which applying advice can have justified force; may remain unresolved or contested.
- *Non-finalizing review*: DEFER/ask source-of-contract is legitimate when evidence genuinely permits opposite judgments; not every unclear case needs a full mathematical equivalence proof.
- *Scientific progress agenda*: empirical record of principle-citation frequency, wrong approval/rejection under matched reviewer access, discrepancy between principle-labeled code and actual project change obligations, effect of new structural policies and reversals.

## Boundary between idea priority and formal proof

**Ideas are allowed before a theorem.** A proof is needed for a universal formal claim; the **problem naming, falsifiable predictions and illustrative counterexamples** can be published as a position/vision/methodological paper when marked as proposed. Do not make a universal undecidability or P≠NP/harder-than-NP assertion from vague difficulty: general semantic equivalence of unrestricted programs can be undecidable, while P vs NP is a different formal complexity question; the actual chosen Go finite-source tests are decidable, bounded and implementable. We neither claim a formal reduction nor compare difficulty orders.

Meyer/Martin are historical interlocutors. Their original advocacy and errors/failures may be scrutinized using concrete current-day evidence; original canonical standing is not authority over new measurements.
