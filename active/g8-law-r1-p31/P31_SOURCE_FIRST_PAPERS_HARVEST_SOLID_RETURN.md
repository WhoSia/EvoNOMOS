## II. Four direct novelty pressure points from full texts

**Geipel & Schweitzer:** 35 Java OSS projects, 33 from SourceForge. There is a dependency–cochange association and change can propagate along dependency paths, *but* about half the dependency edges never cochange; in Eclipse a concentrated minority of edges accounts for most cochanges. Static edges are not weighted equally by historical propagation, and code history is an observed, not experimentally manipulated, demand source. The source's Java/OSS sample and multi-year project selection constrain transport.

**Ajienka et al.:** 79 OO OSS projects, selected from Google Code data. Their identifier-only measures are cheaper but not universally interchangeable with full-class corpora; the strengths of semantic similarity and historical logical (cochange) coupling show no linear association, yet over 70% of semantically connected class pairs also cochange in their sample. Their historical association-rule support/confidence and threshold/IR choice are relevant baseline variables. This *directional overlap* is not a causal obligation or a prediction that changing one design will change lifecycle quality. Do not discard a strong lexical-plus-history baseline.

**Parnas:** A module is a responsibility assignment, not necessarily a function. Information-hiding anticipated change decisions were already the scientific starting point in 1972. Renaming anticipated change as 'obligation' does not create novelty.

**Cai et al. (DRSpaces) and Lohse–Zweben:** overlapping design-rule spaces, persistent architecture roots and bug-proneness are stronger geometry baselines than number of static edges. Lohse & Zweben's three design experiments already found that modification type significantly affected modifiability while module-coupling style had no stable overall effect. Claiming generic demand moderation as unprecedented is untenable.

## V. Research question and competing baselines

**Question:** Before a new demand is implemented, can the requirement-induced *joint obligation and noninterference* pattern predict the conditional signed/vector effect of sharing vs splitting an actual OO responsibility boundary, beyond static DSM/DRSpaces, historical cochange, lexical/semantic coupling, and traditional anticipated-variation analysis?

The important discrimination is not whether cochange occurs, but **whether a demand requires cochange, forbids propagation or requires a new capability**, and whether implementing that requirement under two semantically equivalent structures produces different change effects. Requiring/forbidding changes is a new measurement target, not yet a novel theoretical discovery.

Strong rivals:
1. Parnas change-expectation and design decision hiding.
2. Cai DRSpaces/architecture-root and static/propagation geometry.
3. Historical dependency cochange (Geipel), semantic+history (Ajienka).
4. Traditional pattern/maintainability experiments (including Lohse-Zweben).
5. Simpler number-of-modifications, interface-arity, adapter construction tax.

**A valid next experiment must prospectively separate at least one named baseline's prediction from the candidate, use a complete common functional oracle across source-faithful rivals, and measure the noncollapsed S/L/C/A/Q vector across later demands.** If a semantic+history predictor can account for the same variation, prefer it. LSP remains an admissibility/behavioral substitutability constraint, not a numeric preference.
